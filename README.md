# darkcola_phosphate_inc-blood-calcium
Notes on phosphate consumption leading to increased calcium levels, effects on kidneys/cardiovasc system


Dark colas get their tang from phosphoric acid, and the phosphate in it is almost completely absorbed, compared with roughly 40 to 60 percent from phosphate that occurs naturally in food.

Healthy kidneys clear the excess easily. As kidney function falls, though, phosphate builds up, and that drives bone loss, calcification of blood vessels, and further strain on the kidneys.


When a rapidly absorbed phosphate load hits the blood, it binds some of the free (ionized) calcium, so ionized calcium dips slightly. The parathyroid glands sense this and release PTH. PTH restores blood calcium by pulling it from bone, raising calcium reabsorption in the kidney, and signaling the kidney to dump phosphate. At the same time, bone cells release FGF23, which also pushes phosphate out through the urine but suppresses activation of vitamin D, so your gut absorbs less calcium from food.

  Could explain high PTH levels 


Bone density. The best-known study is from the Framingham Osteoporosis cohort (Tucker and colleagues, 2006). Women who drank cola daily had lower hip bone density than those who drank it less than once a month. Other carbonated drinks didn't show the same link, which pointed at something specific to cola rather than carbonation.

Higher blood phosphate, even within the normal lab range, is associated with more calcification of arteries and heart valves and more cardiovascular events in large population studies. High phosphate can directly convert the smooth muscle cells in vessel walls into bone-forming cells. FGF23, which rises in response to phosphate loads, is independently linked to thickening of the heart muscle.
